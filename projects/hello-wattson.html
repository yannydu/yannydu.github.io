Hello Wattson

Lessons and coding tools for classrooms, opened by an access code in class, with logins coming for our own clubs.

What it is

Hello Wattson is where we publish our teaching material: 22 programs of Arduino, web development, LEGO SPIKE and Python lessons, plus the coding tools students use beside them. A teacher anywhere can browse the public catalogue, then unlock the lessons with an access code they hand to the class. For classroom students the site stores nothing: no accounts, no names, and no saved work on our side. Our own clubs are moving to logins, so we control who gets into each club and members can save their work and take it home. Over 2026 it went from a static notes site, to coding tools in the browser, to a picture-book format with our mascots.

Dev log

Oct 31, 2026: three doors

During the summer camps I noticed the home page getting cluttered once we also offered classroom workshops and camps. I wanted it to be clearer. The program board became three doors, Clubs, Camps and Workshops, each opening its own page. A door shows what is running now, or else what is coming next. Every listed program now names its format and pathway, and the build fails if one is missing a start date or grade band.

Oct 22 to Nov 1, 2026: a picture-book session format

I wanted the site to have a design of its own, with more of the Science Alive mascots in it, giving them more life and stories. Each club session now opens with a story and a mission card, and the idea decks carry concept pictures: small drawings of pins, parts and direction, most of them short animations with a still version. The sessions moved over to this format one at a time.

Oct 18 to Oct 24, 2026: Arduino in the browser

Once the LiveCodes playground was part of the site, we wanted the same consistency for hardware, and an easier way to run hardware workshops in schools, offsite and on other devices. School laptops can’t install the Arduino IDE (integrated development environment), so the editor uses the IDE’s own Verify and Upload buttons. Verify sends the sketch to a compile route that needs an unlocked session and is rate-limited per IP address. A Durable Object holds up to 30 waiting compiles in front of a container running arduino-cli with internet off, and accepts only a short list of libraries. Upload flashes the board over Web Serial with our own uploader, so the editor needs Chrome or Edge. Common compiler errors come back as one plain line naming the line number, with the raw text folded underneath.

I built it Oct 18 to 21. On staging, 25 compiles sent at once waited at most 44 seconds, because one instance compiles one sketch at a time. A flash test on a real Uno passed on Linux; the Windows club laptop and a Chromebook are next. Gadget Club’s first session used it on Oct 24.

Oct 15 to Oct 18, 2026: idea cards, playground first

Pixel Club and Gadget Club are weekly Saturday clubs whose sessions open on their own dates. Their reading pages became idea-card decks: one card shows at a time, with Back and Next, and each card holds at most 40 words. Our lead picked this layout, “One idea at a time”, from several versions. The software clubs also went playground-first: lesson steps no longer mention a local editor, and taking the project home moved to a section at the end of the lesson.

Oct 7, 2026: the Workbench

The Workbench puts the playground beside a Pixel Lab lesson. On screens 1200 pixels wide and up, the lesson takes 42% of the width and the playground the rest. Smaller screens keep a link out to the playground, and phones get no embed. If the embed hasn’t loaded in 8 seconds, the page shows a plain message and the link instead.

Sep 20, 2026: classroom codes and a playground

Classroom codes moved out of git into Workers KV, Cloudflare’s key-value store. Each code lists its modules, a label and an optional expiry, and one command-line tool issues, lists or revokes codes without a redeploy. A LiveCodes playground went up on its own origin, play.hellowattson.ca. Student code runs entirely in the browser and saves to the device.

Jul 10, 2026: Astro 6 and a public catalogue

I moved the site from Eleventy to Astro 6, keeping every existing URL. Our plan called for auto-graded quizzes, interactive lesson widgets and a public catalogue, which Astro handles better, and moving first meant building the catalogue and track templates once. A Cloudflare Worker in front checks the access code on the server before any lesson leaves. The catalogue and undated tracks let a teacher outside our program preview a module and run it on their own schedule, and a plain-language privacy page went up with them.

Mar 9, 2026: a notes site

It started as an Eleventy site, a static site generator, holding notes our own instructors projected in class. I wanted students to have notes they could reach outside camps and classes if they wanted to keep studying, and to give instructors more resources to point students to.

What’s next (planned)

Club logins come first: club members sign in to save a session’s work to our gallery and load it again at home. They work on staging and launch after a privacy review. The rest is not built yet. In order: a teacher layer, with a “For teachers” section per module, teacher downloads scoped to the module a code unlocks, and free preview lessons; self-check quizzes that grade in the browser and store nothing; JupyterLite notebooks for Python; and simulator embeds for the hardware modules.

Constraints

A classroom is thirty devices sharing one school IP address, often on slow wifi, with no time to install anything. Classroom students cannot have accounts. Program pages label themselves upcoming, running, or finished, and those labels have to be right on a morning when nobody has touched the site in weeks.

The no-accounts rule for classrooms is also what lets the site reach outside our own rooms. BC public schools work under FIPPA, the provincial privacy law, and a tool that stores student personal information brings privacy-impact paperwork before a teacher can use it. A site that stores nothing about students gives a district privacy officer one page to read instead of a review. Accounts would give us saved progress and a class view for teachers. They would also make us the keeper of a directory of children. In a classroom the unit of access is a code, and the student stays anonymous to us. Our own clubs are where we take on accounts, because there a login controls who gets into each club and lets members take their work home.

Architecture

The Worker runs ahead of the files it serves, so a gated lesson is never handed out and then hidden. A build served without the Worker would give everything away, which is why every deploy goes through it. The rate limit counts only failed code attempts, so one student mistyping cannot lock out the classmates sharing that school IP address. The playground is a separate Worker with no gate and no secrets, and a first-party subdomain is less likely to be blocked by a school content filter than a third-party domain.
